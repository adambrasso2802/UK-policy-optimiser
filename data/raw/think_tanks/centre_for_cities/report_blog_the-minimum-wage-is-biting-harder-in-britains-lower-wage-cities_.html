The minimum wage is biting harder in Britain’s lower-wage cities

How the National Living Wage affects British cities and why local labour markets need closer attention.

Blog post published on 14 May 2026 by Maxwell Read

The Centre for Cities’ latest report Pay Check: The minimum wage in British cities highlights how the recent rise in the National Living Wage (NLW) affects urban Britain. The recent rise to £12.71 puts the UK’s wage floor among the highest in the OECD, sparking debate about where the minimum wage should go next.

As two-thirds of all jobs are in cities, cities should occupy an important position in assessments of the impact of the minimum wage. While the government is taking a place-based approach to urban labour markets in other areas of policy, a view of cities is missing from the debate on the NLW.

A single national wage looks different across cities

Since 2019, the NLW has set a minimum wage target at two-thirds of median earnings. This target is set based on national data but masks local variation.

The UK is one of the most geographically unequal places in the developed world, creating very different labour markets in different parts of the country. It has also featured the fastest minimum wage increase of the past 20 years of any country in the G7. Figure 1 shows that in 2026, 45 out of 62 British cities and large towns sit above the two-thirds target, mostly outside the Greater South East, compared to just three when the National Living Wage was announced in 2015.

Figure 1: Most UK cities exceed the government’s target ratio

At the extremes, the same minimum wage of £12.71 is 53 per cent of the median in Reading and 82 per cent of the median in Doncaster. Were the latter city its own nation, it would have the third highest minimum wage in the OECD after Colombia and Costa Rica.

While the local bite of the minimum wage has been increasing everywhere, it is increasing faster in high-bite cities. We find that since the minimum wage was introduced in 1999, the variation in the bite between cities has risen by 37 per cent.

Coverage also varies significantly between cities. In Telford, 12.2 per cent of all workers earn the NLW, almost double the national figure of 6.6 per cent. In Oxford, that number is less than 2 per cent.

The NLW is a key factor in cities’ changing wage patterns

Moving up the wage distribution, we see how significant raising the NLW is for many cities.

Rising minimum wages do not just affect those at the minimum; they have broader impacts on organisations’ pay scales. In Southend, almost one-third of all workers are now earning within £1 of the minimum, compared to just over 10 per cent in London.

The strong wage growth at the bottom of the pay scale induced by the NLW, coupled with the stagnation of national wages in the rest of the economy, is causing significant wage compression in all but a handful of cities. In cities with a high minimum wage bite, the gaps between wage deciles in the bottom half of the pay scale are narrowing much faster, suggesting that the overall impact of the minimum wage in these more constrained labour markets is rising. Figure 2 shows that wage compression at the bottom end of the wage distribution is strongly related to the local bite. While the top end is also compressing due to broader economic issues, this is less closely related to the minimum wage.

Figure 2: The minimum wage is driving wage compression at the bottom end of the wage distribution

Minimum wage has yet to cause severe harm, but risks remain

Economists have generally concluded that minimum wages have so far not harmed employment and have been effective in raising living standards. But what is also clear is that the higher the minimum wage is relative to earnings, the riskier this policy is. Other potential side effects, such as poorer working conditions, reductions in worker training or falling firm profitability, may also occur where minimum wages are too high relative to local economies.

Those risks are likely to be greatest in high-bite cities in the North and Midlands. The government has acknowledged the need to raise employment in underperforming regions; any threat to that should be accounted for. Given the level of the bite in parts of the North, we advise caution when it comes to further above-inflation increases in the NLW.

This risk is evidenced by rising youth unemployment, which has halted the proposed increase in the separate minimum wage set for workers under 21. We find that the local bite of the minimum wage is correlated with the percentage of youth seeking in-work benefits.

Better spatial thinking about the minimum wage is needed

The government needs a minimum wage strategy that is better aligned with the place-based focus of the rest of its labour market policy. The disparity in the NLW’s impact on cities necessitates a cautious approach towards future rises.

The Low Pay Commission (LPC), the statutory body tasked with recommending minimum wage rates since the policy’s inception, has assessed the NLW from many angles, including its effects on different employers, sectors, and age groups. But its consideration of geography is locked into a regional view that fails to account for the variation between cities.

The government should give the LPC an explicit remit from 2027 to consider the local labour market impacts of the minimum wage, focusing on urban areas. Within this broader remit, the LPC should fill evidence gaps in how local labour markets interact with non-employment effects and the impacts on particular sectors or demographics.
