Tradeable jobs and city centre regeneration: lessons from Wakefield

What Wakefield’s logistics-led growth means for wages, footfall and plans for city-centre regeneration.

Blog post published on 15 April 2026 by Oscar Selby

Recently, Centre for Cities visited Wakefield. Alongside discovering the pivotal role the city plays in the Rhubarb Triangle, we learned about how the council is planning to develop the city centre into a place for more people to live and work. Three lessons rang true:

  1. Tradeable jobs in logistics and manufacturing support employment and living standards.
  2. But they locate on the edge of town and pay lower wages, limiting the benefit to the city centre.
  3. Attracting residents and businesses into the city centre offers a possible solution.

Tradeable jobs in logistics and manufacturing have increased employment and living standards in Wakefield

Over the last decade, Wakefield has been one of the UK’s cities where output and disposable income have grown faster than the national average. This is particularly impressive considering it is in a part of the country that has struggled with de-industrialisation and low employment rates over the last few decades.

Figure 1: In Wakefield output and disposable income have grown faster than the national average

Central to Wakefield’s relative economic success has been the creation of new jobs for its residents: between 2013 and 2023, twice as many jobs per resident were created in Wakefield relative to the average UK city. Figure 2 shows that most of Wakefield’s new jobs were in logistics and manufacturing – tradeable exporting industries that bring money into local economies and  support economic development.

Figure 2: This has been enabled by jobs growth in tradeable industries like logistics and manufacturing

Together, logistics and manufacturing accounted for 52 per cent of the new jobs created in Wakefield and over 90 per cent of new tradeable jobs. Growth in logistics has been made possible by the city’s location between Sheffield and Leeds and its position on the East Coast Main Line railway. It has also been facilitated by the wider technological shift towards online shopping over the last decade, which has supported significant growth in the industry.

But it is not just geographical convenience and macro trends that have facilitated the growth in logistics and manufacturing jobs. By releasing land for development in Normanton Industrial Estate and Wakefield Industrial Park, the council has helped these sectors to grow.

These jobs locate on the edge of town and pay relatively lower wages, limiting their contribution to reviving the city centre

Jobs in logistics and manufacturing have their strengths. As well as being tradeable jobs, they are often accessible to people without a university degree. So, they can support living standards in places where higher-level qualifications are less common.

But jobs in manufacturing and logistics tend to locate on the edge of town, where land is more available and less expensive. Figure 3 shows that the vast majority of jobs in logistics and manufacturing are in three industrial estates located far beyond the city centre.

Figure 3: Jobs in logistics and manufacturing tend to be outside the city centre

Source: Business Registry and Employment Survey; Automatic Knowledge

Because Wakefield’s tradeable jobs are located on its periphery, daytime footfall in the city centre is lower than it would be if the jobs were located nearby. This limits the multiplier effects they generate and weakens demand for additional goods and services.

Jobs in logistics and manufacturing also tend to pay relatively lower salaries. Despite Wakefield’s growth in living standards recently, workplace wages grew more slowly than in the average UK city. It was an increase in employment, not an increase in pay, that drove disposable income growth in Wakefield.

To raise wages and support the city centre, Wakefield is trying to attract in residents and businesses

To support higher wages, the council is working to support more jobs in higher-paying, advanced manufacturing businesses. Alongside this, Wakefield Council is planning to repopulate the city centre. With anchor retailers like Primark and M&S moving out, the council is going to use £17.9 million from the Ministry of Housing, Communities and Local Government to acquire the Ridings Shopping Centre, demolish it, and build 1,000 new homes in its place.

The ambition is to attract more of Wakefield’s existing and future residents to live in the city centre to support more footfall on the high street and increase demand for goods and services. The council is also looking to take advantage of the city’s geography and provide commuter housing for people who have been priced out of Leeds.

By increasing the number of higher-qualified workers and providing new office space via developments like Tileyard North, the council is also hoping to attract knowledge-intensive businesses into the city centre to raise wages and provide even more daytime footfall.

Similar strategies, supporting out-of-town manufacturing and logistics jobs while attracting residents and businesses into the city centre, are already proving fruitful in places like Barnsley and Warrington. If the council in Wakefield is able to deliver on its ambitions, then it should support higher-paying jobs and more vibrant city-centre high streets in the future.
