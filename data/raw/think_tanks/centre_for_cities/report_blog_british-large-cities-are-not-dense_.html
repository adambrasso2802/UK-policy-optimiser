Britain’s large cities are not dense

Why Britain’s big cities need denser urban cores to boost productivity, transport and local economic growth.

Blog post published on 16 April 2026 by Anthony Breach

The government thinks Britain’s big cities are not dense enough. The government’s Northern Growth Strategy identifies that “increasing the density of northern cities is essential to unlocking agglomeration benefits and driving productivity growth”. To that end, it has launched a City Densification Fund of £800 million, is supporting New Town proposals to densify parts of Manchester and Leeds, and is trying to get more dense development around stations.

But sometimes it’s claimed that British cities are already dense.

Population rings show that British big cities are large

When using a population ring – the total number of people within a circle – Britain’s big cities are surprisingly similar to cities abroad that we might at first think of as much denser. Within a 20-kilometre ring around the city centre, Manchester has roughly the same population as Kyiv, Rome and Warsaw. The area within a 20-kilometre ring of Leeds has a similar population to Lyon, Vancouver and Turin.

But does this mean these big British cities are already as dense as these global peers? Do we not need to bother trying to densify them?

Density comparisons need to look within cities and at buildings

Part of the issue is that it’s surprisingly tricky to compare density between cities, even though it’s often straightforward to tell whether one neighbourhood is denser than another.

For international, city-wide comparisons of density, there are two key things to consider separately from the total population of the urban area.

First, we need to understand how density varies within cities. Ideally, cities should be denser closer to city centres, as more valuable land should be shared between more people who prioritise proximity and short commutes. If densities are relatively low close to city centres, that’s bad for the city’s transport accessibility.

Second, we should try to look at the amount of building floorspace in a city. Policymakers have much more control over how many buildings can be built within a neighbourhood than over how many people decide to live within it. If a city has lots of people squeezing into overcrowded accommodation, that’s a poor outcome which suggests more and denser buildings are needed locally.

British big cities have low-density urban cores – between their centres and their suburbs

We can look at these by using the same data we developed for our report Flat Britain – which calculates floorspace per hectare by combining data from both national censuses and Global Human Settlement Layer data. This takes the average British dwelling’s amount of residential floorspace and uses it to compare the density of French, Japanese and British neighbourhoods, and makes possible comparisons to ‘pair’ cities of a similar population that we had identified in Flat Britain.

If we show this data cumulatively in Figure 1 – essentially showing a population ring kilometre by kilometre – we can see that the biggest English cities outside London really do have comparable numbers of homes to similar French and Japanese cities within a 13-kilometre distance from their centre. But they have far fewer homes than French and Japanese cities in neighbourhoods within 4.5 kilometres of the centre.

Figure 1: British big cities have less dense urban cores than similarly sized cities abroad

This is the problem facing British big cities. They have low densities not in the immediate city centre, where policy has often facilitated new high-density development. Instead, their low-density neighbourhoods are in this much larger ‘urban core’ area between 2 and 4.5 kilometres from the city centre. Neighbourhoods in these urban cores are crucial for densification, as while they have good public transport accessibility, the low density of their built environment limits their population and the effective size of their cities’ labour markets.

Focusing on the urban core explains what’s missing from the population rings. Within 5 kilometres, Manchester is much smaller than Kyiv, Rome and Warsaw. Similarly, within 5 kilometres, Leeds is smaller than Lyon, Vancouver and Turin.

More density helps make the case for the big cities

From this, there are two lessons. First, the government is right to want to close the density gap in the big cities. More homes and more workers means bigger local and national economies, and will help tackle the country’s regional divides.

Second, densification makes it easier to deliver other kinds of investment. The argument for better public transport in the big cities becomes much stronger if more passengers can be guaranteed. More workers within a reasonable commute makes it easier to secure commercial property investment and create more jobs. And local politicians taking the lead on big changes to existing residential neighbourhoods will make the case for city devolution inarguable.
